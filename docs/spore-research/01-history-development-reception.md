# 1. Origins, Development History, Release, Business & Reception

> Scope: how *Spore* (Maxis / Electronic Arts, 2008) was conceived, built, shown, delayed, shipped, sold, reviewed and argued about, and what happened to it afterwards. Figures are from the sources listed at the end. Where sources disagree, the best-supported figure is given first and the range is noted.

## 1.2 Origins and concept (c. 2000–2004)

### Working title: "SimEverything"
- Wright's first name for the project was **"Sim Everything"** (also written *SimEverything*). The New Yorker's 2006 profile says: "At first, Wright called the project Sim Everything, but a few years ago he settled on the name Spore."
- **"Spore" was a working title suggested by Maxis art director Ocean Quigley**, and it stuck. Wright said dropping the "Sim" prefix was liberating: "Not putting 'Sim' in front of it was very refreshing to me. It feels like it wants to be breaking out into a completely different thing than what Sim was."
- A Wikipedia image caption calls an early "SimEverything" poster a 1994 concept. Every other source dates active development to around 2000, so the 1994 date should be treated cautiously.

### The two big ideas: *Powers of Ten* and Drake's equation
- Wright told The New Yorker that the key "landmarks" for Spore were not books, as with his earlier games (*SimEarth* drew on Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis, *SimAnt* on E. O. Wilson's *The Ants*). They were **Drake's equation** (Frank Drake, 1961) and **Charles and Ray Eames's 1977 film *Powers of Ten***. In his words: "I love 'The Powers of Ten,' … I've always been a big fan of the Eameses. At the same time, I am really interested in the terms of Drake's equation, and when I began working on Spore I was using it to map some of the game play. At some point I realized that the terms of Drake's equation mapped neatly to the scale of 'The Powers of Ten.' So I rolled the two up into Spore."
- The earliest version grew out of an interest in **SETI**: "The original concept was sort of a toy galaxy you could fly around and explore." Lead designer Chris Trottier recalled that Wright first wanted a game about the Drake equation and the improbability of the universe. At GDC 2005 Wright cited an earlier article he had written about a SETI game as the basis for the space-scale design. At E3 2008 he called the SETI Institute a major inspiration.
- In a 2008 GamesBeat interview Wright also named *2001: A Space Odyssey*, SETI, *Powers of Ten*, and books on astronomy and evolution. Wikipedia adds TV shows, films and toys such as **Lego** and ***Star Wars***.
- **Procedural content** was the second pillar. Soren Johnson's postmortem names the project's "two big ideas" as *powers of ten* and *procedural content*.

### Game-genre homages, stage by stage
Each stage was conceived as a nod to a classic genre. Different sources give slightly different lists:

| Stage (final name) | Homage named by sources |
|---|---|
| Cell (earlier "tide pool") | *Pac-Man* (all sources; GDC 2005 called it an "analog Pac-Man") |
| Creature | *Diablo* |
| Tribal | *Populous* (the DICE 2007 slide likened Tribal to *Populous: The Beginning*) |
| City (cut) | *SimCity* |
| Civilization | *Civilization* (Johnson); "Risk/Civ" (Don Hopkins's GDC notes) |
| Space | *Master of Orion* (Johnson; "MOO" at GDC 2005). *Space Invaders* also appeared on the GDC 2005 list |

- Other influences recorded from the GDC 2005 talk include ***Katamari Damacy*** and ***Pikmin*** (on Wright's design graph), ***Pokémon*** (creature design), *Neopets*, *Care Bears*, Wright's childhood love of dinosaurs, and ***Myst*** (as an example of art-heavy development).
- **The demoscene** was a major technical influence. Wright hired demoscene programmers and artists for their procedural-generation skills and showed pictures from demoparties such as Assembly at GDC 2005, to applause.
- The *Sporepedia* was described as inspired by **Web 2.0**.
- Dennett's *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* was invoked by The New Yorker when describing the game's algorithmic view of life. Commentators compared the creature editor to Richard Dawkins's *Biomorphs* (*The Blind Watchmaker*), but this research found no direct Wright statement naming Dawkins or *Star Control* as influences.

### Early years
- Development began **around 2000**, roughly when *The Sims Online* started. Wright told GamesBeat he started researching about seven years before launch and formed a core team about five years before. He compared that to *The Sims*, which he prototyped about seven years before it shipped.
- **February 2004:** Wright secretly published a Spore diagram, laid out as a cycle of evolution, in *Wired*. Neither the magazine nor readers knew what it was.
- **Chris Hecker** joined Maxis in 2004. His first documented Spore work, on how skin would drape over arbitrary creations, dates from late 2003. Associate producer Guillaume Pierre first saw the design document in 2003.

## 1.3 The reveal: GDC 2005 "The Future of Content"

- **Date:** 11 March 2005, the final day of GDC 2005 in San Francisco. Gamasutra's report is dated 15 March 2005.
- **Secrecy:** According to Don Hopkins's notes, the vague title was deliberate. Wright wanted to show Spore to developers before a commercial show like E3, and he sent **a decoy set of slides to EA executives** so they would not know what he planned. The 2026 Design Room oral history says EA executive **Don Mattrick warned against showing the game so early** and Wright overruled him.
- **Argument:** "Content and cost do not directly translate into value." Procedural methods could break the rising content-cost curve, and player ownership was a third axis. "Player stories will always be more powerful than scripted stories." Wright claimed procedural systems could reach **"5000 to 1 compression."** He described the early stages as "tutorials for the eventual sandbox."
- **Stages demonstrated (per Gamasutra):**
  1. **Cell:** a single-celled organism in a primordial soup, evolved by adding parts in an editor ("analog Pac-Man").
  2. **Ocean/underwater:** a 3D underwater world populated asynchronously with other players' creatures.
  3. **Land/creature:** the three-legged creature fans later named the **"Willosaur."** It hunted, fed, fled predators, mated, played a drum and danced, all procedurally animated. Pre-made creatures included a multi-headed six-legged insectoid, "Tweety Bird the SUV," and a Care Bear-like "vicious carnivore."
  4. **Tribal:** sentience, fire, huts, music.
  5. **City:** a SimCity-like level with trading and buildings and vehicles.
  6. **UFO:** abducting creatures and terraforming with a "Genesis device," then reaching an interstellar drive.
  7. **Galaxy/space:** black holes, nebulae, alien radio, first contact, hostile aliens.
- Sources count the 2005 phases differently. The English Wikipedia lists seven (Cell, Underwater, Creature, Tribe, City, Civilization, Space) and the Spore Wiki lists six.
- **Reception:** a sustained standing ovation. A flying creature was briefly visible, and the Willosaur became an early mascot.
- At the same GDC (and at Futureplay), Chris Hecker gave "Why you should have paid attention in multivariable calculus," on implicit surfaces and texture projection for Spore's creatures.
- In hindsight, art director Ocean Quigley told the Design Room oral history that a journalist had asked whether the demo was the greatest game design ever or "an act of bold chicanery." His own answer: "It was B." Wright admitted "We were definitely overrepresenting what it eventually became there," and Alex Hutchinson said it "built a fantasy in people's minds that was unachievable." Senior producer Morgan Roarty took the opposite line at launch in 2008. He said most of the demo's systems shipped ("It's pretty close"), though procedural animation was still "a research project" when he joined just before GDC 2005.

### Delay history
- The game appeared at **three consecutive E3s (2005–2007)** with a promised release that year.
- **Original target:** Wright told Popular Science in Feb 2007 that he was still aiming for fall 2007.
- **May 2007:** Riccitiello pushed it to Q4 FY08 (Jan–Mar 2008) or Q1 FY09 (Apr–Jun 2008). CFO Warren Jenson excluded it from FY08 guidance.
- **1 Aug 2007:** "squarely targeted against March, April, May of next year." In **Oct 2007** Wright said roughly six months (about April 2008).
- **29 Jan 2008:** Gamasutra reported a possible slip to fall or winter 2008. Riccitiello then said "before the holidays." **12 Feb 2008:** September dates announced.
- Lucy Bradshaw told GamesBeat in 2008 that the game was "a year late" but the extra time had gone into polish. The 2018 oral history says the team got "an extra year" to tie the separate games together, including how aggressive, passive or mixed play in early stages determines later archetypes (shaman, warrior, trader) through a formula.

- **75** (New Yorker, 2006: "the seventy-five-member team" in Emeryville).
- **80** (Lucy Bradshaw, 2008: 80 people for the PC game, the Mac version and the community site. DS and mobile were separate teams).
- **"More than 100"** (Wright, GamesBeat, Sept 2008). The 2018 oral history and the 2026 Design Room oral history say "over 100," spread across two buildings.

### Prototypes and technology
- Maxis posted **fourteen prototypes** publicly (Johnson). Shacknews reported the release on 9 Sept 2008, and they had been online for about a month before. Names included *ParticleMan*, *SPUG*, *City Maze*, *Space* (20 MB) and *Gonzago* (45 MB).
- **Procedural creature data:** Wright said at E3 2006 that a creature took "a couple of kilobytes," calling it "the DNA template of a creature while the game, like a womb, builds the 'phenotypes.'"
- **Procedural animation:** Hecker's GDC 2007 abstract: "Spore has a unique problem: most of the content in the game will be made after the game ships." Animators keyframed motion in a morphology-independent form in the in-house tool **"Spasm,"** and it was retargeted at runtime to arbitrary player skeletons. This was published as a SIGGRAPH 2008 paper, "Real-time Motion Retargeting to Highly Varied User-Created Morphologies." Wright later said Hecker's work had advanced the state of the art in procedural animation by several years. Johnson notes procedural animation was not finished until the final stretch.

## 1.6 Design philosophy and how the shipped game diverged

### "Cute" vs. "Science"
- Wright said the team split into two camps. The **"Cute" camp** wanted a Sims-like, accessible and emotionally engaging game. The **"Science" camp** wanted realism. The final game was a compromise: "We ended up with a very nice balance of the two factors." Johnson describes a "tenuous compromise" already in place when he arrived in 2007.
- Seed magazine (quoted by ScienceBlogs, Nov 2008) quoted Hecker: "I was the founding member of the 'cute' team… Ocean [Quigley] and Will were really the founding members of the 'science' team. Ocean would make the cell game look exactly like a petri dish with all these to-scale animals and Will would say, 'That's the greatest thing I've ever seen!'" Seed reported that prototyping teams had been "becoming lost in their scientific interests."
- Seed also reported that the original concept was **more scientifically accurate**. It had realistic art for single-celled organisms and **rejected faster-than-light travel** as impossible. Both were dropped to make the game friendlier to casual players.
- Some players read Hecker's Seed remarks as making him responsible for the loss of hard science. Wikipedia says Wright and Bradshaw discredited that interpretation.
- The most visible result was the **art-style shift** from the "gritty" GDC 2005 microbes and animals to rounder, softer creatures, with cartoon eyes on cells "to make it cute" (Wright, TED 2007).
- Wright on the player's role: "It's funny because in the game you're in the role of an intelligent designer. Yet the meta-message of the game is that life becomes what it is through the process of evolution." He also said early mutation-driven prototypes lacked emotional engagement, which is why the player was made the designer.

### Stage structure over time

| Date | Phases listed |
|---|---|
| GDC 2005 | Cell, Underwater, Creature, Tribe, City, (Civilization), Space (UFO and galaxy) |
| DICE, Feb 2007 | Eight: Molecular, Cell, Creature, Tribal, City, Civilization, Terraform, Galactic |
| TED, Mar 2007 | Five: Molecular merged into Cell, City absorbed into Civilization, Terraform and Galactic re-merged into Space |
| Release, Sept 2008 | **Cell, Creature, Tribal, Civilization, Space** |

### Features cut or reduced
- **Aquatic/Underwater stage:** shown at GDC 2005. PC Gamer UK (July 2006) even previewed underwater civilizations. Wright said at SXSW 2007 it was nearly cut and could return as an expansion. It shipped cut. Swimming is limited to the surface, and creatures that stray too far out are eaten by a sea monster. Wikipedia says a later DLC idea, "The Depths," was dropped over animation, navigation, camera and parts problems.
- **Molecular stage:** a *Tetris*-like stage before Cell, later merged into Cell.
- **City stage:** a city-design phase between Tribe and Civilization, dropped as too large in scope. Roarty said in Sept 2008 it could "possibly" return.
- **Flight:** creatures got limited **gliding** only, based on jump and glide stats. Wright's Gadgetoff 2007 bird creature only flapped.
- **Procedural mating and calls:** Don Hopkins's favourite feature from the 2005 notes, with reproduction as currency. It was cut.
- **Creature-stage starting point:** the 2006 E3 video said players would start as a slug-like animal. The shipped game links Cell to Creature by emerging from the water.
- **Granularity of evolution:** in the shipped Creature stage, parts carry fixed stats regardless of placement. Johnson's example is the "Stubbtoe" foot: Sprint 2, Dance 1, Speed 2 wherever it is attached. In the Cell editor, placement of proboscis, flagella and cilia does affect play. Johnson traces this to an unresolved dispute: "an interactive art museum or a customizable video game?"

### Criticism of shallow stages, and Wright's answer
- Wright, Sept 2008 (GamesBeat): he expected criticism of the depth of the levels, which was "pretty much intentional"; "Intentionally, if you go after a lot of people who don't play games at all, you will take hits among the hardcore gamers." He added: "We'd rather have the Sims 2 sales and the lower Metacritic rating than Half-Life 2's sales." To MTV the same week he said: "We were very focused, if anything, on making a game for more casual players… we decided — quite a while back — that we would rather have the Metacritic and sales of *Sims 2* than the Metacritic and sales of *Half-Life*." He said *Sims 2* scored around 90 and *Half-Life* 97.
- Easy, medium and hard difficulty levels were added late. A cheat unlocks all stages so players can skip ones they dislike (Roarty).
- Wright later accepted the main critique that the game was "five distinct games amalgamated rather haphazardly" (Design Room, 2026). In 2022 he told Axios it would probably have been better as "three games or four" that players could intersect.

### Postmortems
**Soren Johnson, "Spore: My View of the Elephant" (30 Sept 2013, Designer Notes).** Johnson saw about 20% of the project.
1. *Don't be afraid to challenge the initial vision.* "Making five different games at once is a bad idea," and "only one of these ideas was any good." He means procedural content, not powers of ten. Shared verbs also fragmented across stages: friendship meant dancing in the Creature stage and attacking cities with music in the Civilization stage. He would have limited scope to the biological stages.
2. *Gameplay must support the theme.* The game was marketed as being about evolution but was really about creativity. He quotes *Science*: "When it comes to biology, and particularly evolution, Spore failed miserably." It was "at its best when played by ten-year-olds."
3. *The only prototype which matters is the game.* "100 compelling yet limited prototypes are less likely to lead to a great game than a single playable one." The game was not fully playable until, at best, the final year. Delaying further was "politically and emotionally impossible."
4. *Team cohesion beats team quality.* Beyond "cute vs. science," there was a split between a newer **"gameplay" team** (which later drove *Darkspore*) and the older **"Sim" team** (toy box and self-expression). "Making a game for everybody is the same thing as making a game for nobody." Stone Librande recalled Johnson saying: "We made the best kid's game ever. Too bad we weren't trying to make a kid's game." Henry Goffin wrote that Maxis's structure "doomed Spore from the start."

**"Developing Spore: an oral ('Sporal'?) history 10 years on" (Game Developer, 11 Sept 2018)**, with Trottier, Compton, Librande, Culyba, Moskowitz and Pierre:
- The team was split into creature, tribe, civ, space and editor teams. Games were "built in silos" with weak connections (Librande).
- The team was competitive and focused on "local excellence" (Trottier). Maxis felt like a research lab (Moskowitz). The schedule seemed both "an infinity away from being done and always just about to ship" (Culyba).
- Compton argued against "hard Darwinism," using the BattleBots wedge-bot example, so that odd creatures would not be punished.

**Design Room oral history (Jay Castello, ~June 2026; covered by PC Gamer and GamesIndustry.biz):**
- The team grew rapidly to over 100 across two locations with limited publisher guidance.
- Quigley said the team "never really identified a central, evolving gameplay loop" and felt no sense of crisis.
- Hutchinson said there was no formal design process and that Wright's part-time presence complicated decisions.
- Chalmers said Wright had "said from the jump that this was going to be a lot of little minigames. Just nobody listened."
- Hecker said "We never felt pressure… EA's got lots of problems, but this was not one of them."

### Platforms and ports
- **Windows and Mac OS X on the same disc.** EA announced the Mac version at Macworld (15 Jan 2008). It was ported by **TransGaming using Cider**, a Wine-derived translation layer, required an Intel Mac, and did not support GMA 950 graphics. Unlike EA's 2007 Mac releases it shipped in the same box on the same day. Wright told Engadget "Mac users will love Spore."
- **Same-day spin-offs:** *Spore Creatures* (DS, Creature-stage RPG) and *Spore Origins* (mobile and iPhone, Cell-stage, *flOw*-like). *Spore Origins* won DICE's 2009 Cellular Game of the Year.
- **Consoles:** Wright repeatedly said "we are going to go on all platforms." A Wii game was confirmed in early design. That became *Spore Hero* (Wii, 2009), alongside *Spore Hero Arena* (DS, 2009). Xbox 360 and PS3 versions were considered and never made.
- **Later digital releases:**
  - **Steam**, without SecuROM: Shacknews dates EA's Steam launch to **19 Dec 2008** at $49.99, alongside the *Creepy & Cute* pack at $20. CNET and Wikipedia say **22 Dec 2008**.
  - **GOG.com**, DRM-free *Spore Collection* (base game, *Galactic Adventures*, *Creepy & Cute*): on sale by **Sept 2016** (Destructoid, 22 Sept 2016). It was later added to GOG's Preservation Program, and the current listing requires Windows 10 or later.
  - **EA's Origin and EA app.** Spore has also appeared in the EA Play catalogue on Steam.
  - The **Mac version stopped working with macOS 10.15 Catalina**, which dropped 32-bit apps. EA forum staff said there was no 64-bit version.

### Editions and pricing
- **Standard:** $49.99 MSRP, ESRB E10+. The Spore Wiki says the base price was later cut to $30.
- ***Spore: Galactic Edition*** (announced 24 June 2008, $79.99, released alongside the standard edition): game DVD; "The Making of Spore" DVD; National Geographic Channel's ~50-minute documentary *How to Build a Better Being* (missing from some regions, such as Asia-Pacific excluding Japan, and Brazil); *The Art of Spore* hardback mini-book (the Spore Wiki says 128 pages); a 100-page Prima "Galactic Handbook"; a fold-out poster; all in a "Spore Glyphics" plastic box. The Spore Wiki says it sold poorly and was cut to $40.

## 1.8 *Spore Creature Creator* (June 2008)

- **Release:** 17 June 2008 (Metacritic and Wikipedia; Europe) and 18 June 2008 (North America, per the Spore Wiki). ESRB rated it E in March 2008. Buechner had said it would come "two to three months before the launch."
- **Versions:** paid full version at **$9.95** (Wikipedia; some previews said $9.99). A **free trial with 25% of the parts**, downloadable from spore.com and bundled with *The SimCity Box*. An AT&T Edition. In Australia, three News Limited papers gave it away free or for $2 on 30 Aug 2008. Metacritic: **83** (7 reviews).

## 1.9 The SecuROM DRM controversy

### Before launch
- In May 2008 BioWare's Derek French said *Mass Effect* PC and *Spore* would use SecuROM with **online re-authentication every 10 days**. After a backlash EA dropped this (announced to the Spore community by Caryl Shaw, per the Spore Wiki). Authentication moved to install and first launch, plus online features, downloads and patches.

### At launch
- **Activation limit: 3 machines per key.** Hardware changes or reinstalls could use up slots, and uninstalling did not restore them. More activations were available "case by case" from EA support. SecuROM was **not disclosed** on the box, in the manual or in the EULA. EA: "we don't disclose specifically which copy protection or digital rights management system we use."
- **Amazon review-bombing:**
  - **10 Sept 2008 (The Register):** 1,662 of 1,797 ratings were one-star.
  - **14 Sept 2008 (TechCrunch/Washington Post):** 2,016 of 2,216.
  - Reviews were briefly removed and the rating reset. Amazon called it a "site glitch" and restored them within hours.
- **EA's defence** (Mariam Sughayer, 12 Sept 2008): fewer than 25% of customers activate a PC title on more than one machine and fewer than 1% try more than three. The model was compared to iTunes. Reports of spyware were "absolutely false." Wikipedia also cites an EA survey: 14% activated on more than one PC and under 1% on more than three. Former Maxis developer Chris Harris called the DRM a "screw up" and a "totally avoidable disaster."
- **Piracy:** a cracked copy appeared about two days before the Australian release, and the DRM was cracked within a day of the 4 Sept launch. TorrentFreak counted ~500,000 BitTorrent downloads within 10 days ("record breaking"), and Forbes estimated 500,000 in the first week. By 4 Dec 2008 TorrentFreak ranked it **the most-pirated PC game of 2008 at ~1.7 million downloads**, ahead of *The Sims 2* (1.15M) and *Assassin's Creed* (1.07M). EA disputed the figures. PaidContent (19 Sept 2008) reported speculation that the botched launch could cost up to **$25 million** in lost sales. Peer-to-peer research firm BigChampagne called the speed "extraordinary."

### Concessions
- **~19 Sept 2008:** EA Games label president **Frank Gibeau** said EA was "disappointed by the misunderstanding surrounding the use of DRM software." EA would **raise the limit to 5 machines** and fast-track de-authorization. EA also reported at least 435,000 activations as a "sample" of sales.
- **De-Authorization Tool:** released around **19 Dec 2008**, which PCWorld called "three months late." It frees a machine slot so another computer can use it, but the 5-activation total remained and the tool was **not available for Mac**.
- **Steam version without SecuROM** (Dec 2008, Steam's own DRM only). **DRM-free GOG version** (by 2016).

### Lawsuits
- **22 Sept 2008:** class action filed in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California by KamberEdelson for Maryland resident **Melissa Thomas**. It alleged violations of California's Consumer Legal Remedies Act and Unfair Competition Law for failing to disclose SecuROM, and complained that SecuROM behaved like a rootkit and stayed after uninstall. It sought the purchase price plus actual, statutory or treble damages. Press put aggregate claims above $5 million.
- **14 Oct 2008:** a second suit (Pennsylvania plaintiff Richard Eldridge) over SecuROM in the **free Creature Creator trial**. A third concerned *The Sims 2: Bon Voyage*.
- **Outcome:** this research found no reported ruling or settlement.

## 1.12 Controversies beyond DRM

### Scientific accuracy and evolution
- ***Science*, "Flunking Spore"** (John Bohannon, *Science* 322(5901):531, 24 Oct 2008). A panel graded the game on **22 subjects**. Evolutionary biologists **T. Ryan Gregory** (Guelph) and **Niles Eldredge** (AMNH) reviewed the Cell and Creature stages. NSF sociologist **William Sims Bainbridge** reviewed the Tribal and Civilization stages. NASA's **Miles Smith** reviewed Space. Grades ranged from an **A for galactic structure** and **B+ for sociology** to **Fs in mutation, sexual selection, natural selection, genetics and genetic drift**. Gregory: "a very impressive, entertaining, and elaborate Mr. Potato Head" that "uses the language of evolution but none of the major principles." Also: "When you run into other members of your species, they are always identical clones of you."
- **Specific objections:** no common descent, since species have separate ancestors. Linear rather than branching change. Directed progress toward intelligence. Change driven by stats and "whatever the player thinks looks cool" rather than selection. Collecting parts from other creatures. Growth in the Cell stage purely by eating.
- **Carl Zimmer** (NYT, 1 Sept 2008, "Gaming Evolves") found biologists' views mixed but positive. Yale's **Thomas Near** and **Richard Prum** enjoyed it but called its evolution "severely messed up." Intelligent-design advocate **Michael Behe** said it "has nothing to do with real science or real evolution—neither Darwinian nor intelligent design."
- **Seed** magazine warned that scientists privately worried the game "seems to further the idea of intelligent design under the badge of science." Studies of players nonetheless suggest the game prompted more sophisticated thinking about evolution than its own model.
- The National Geographic documentary and SETI tie-ins were part of an educational push.

### "Sporn" and content moderation
- Within days of the Creature Creator's release, players made creatures with oversized genitalia or in sexual poses, dubbed **"Sporn."** The Spore Wiki credits the coinage to Kieron Gillen of Rock, Paper, Shotgun on 18 June 2008. Wired covered YouTube uploads the same day.
- EA emailed creators of pornographic machinima and flagged accounts for **ToS violations**, and YouTube removed videos. The "Bad Baby!" achievement was removed in Patch 1.02 because it was seen as encouraging Sporn. Wright told the Associated Press he was not offended.
- **In-game controls:** players can choose to download no user content, Maxis-approved content only, friends' content, or all content. They can ban any creation, and Maxis moderates and bans repeat offenders.

### YouTube integration
- Through an EA–YouTube agreement, the Creature Creator and game could **upload videos directly to YouTube**, and EA ran **"The Spore YouTube Channel."** Patch 1.02 made uploads private by default (after the "Sporn" videos) and Patch 1.05 made them public again.

## 1.14 The end of an era and the game's status today

- **8 April 2009 — Will Wright leaves EA/Maxis** to run **Stupid Fun Club**, an "entertainment think tank" in which EA and Wright held equal stakes and EA had rights to game concepts. Riccitiello said *The Sims*, *SimCity* and Spore would continue. Bradshaw called it an "evolution" of the relationship.
- **26 Aug 2009 — Maxis layoffs**, estimated at "dozens." EA said it was "fully committed to Spore." Hecker was laid off in late 2009.
- **Sept 2011:** Soren Johnson left EA for Zynga.
- **4 March 2015 — EA closes Maxis Emeryville** (~100 staff), the studio behind Spore and the 2013 *SimCity*. It consolidated Maxis IP work in Redwood Shores, Salt Lake City, Helsinki and Melbourne. Former designer Guillaume Pierre's farewell tweet (#RIPMaxisEmeryville) broke the news during GDC. Commentators called it the end of Maxis as it had been.
- **Sept 2015:** Spore's servers were moved to a new datacenter with fresh SSL certificates after connection outages (a Maxis forum post reported by Sims Community, 9 Sept 2015).
- **12 Sept–1 Oct 2024 — revival of support:**
  - An official **Spore Discord** was created on 12 Sept 2024.
  - A post on spore.com (the first in years) announced **two new developers**.
  - Maxis then said it had "not currently [any] plans for major updates to the game itself at this time, or any kind of sequel. Spore is a unique and special game and it is our intent to honor its history." Its "primary focus" was "community engagement."
  - Community posts said a team was again maintaining the servers and renewing certificates.
- **1 Jan 2026:** Hidden Palace's prototype release (see 1.4).
- **June 2026:** the Design Room oral history.
- **Current availability (as of the sources):**
  - **Steam:** base game, *Galactic Adventures* and *Creepy & Cute*. Steam purchases launch directly from Steam and do not appear in the EA app.
  - **GOG:** DRM-free *Spore Collection*, Windows 10 or later.
  - **EA app**, formerly Origin.
  - The Mac version runs only up to macOS Mojave (10.14).
  - Online Sporepedia sharing was still operating as of the 2024 revival.
- **Legacy and successor projects:** players' calls for a "Spore 2" closer to the GDC 2005 vision led to fan projects such as the open-source ***Thrive*** (in development since 2009). Commentators also compare *The Sapling*, *The Universim*, *Niche*, *No Man's Sky*, *Kerbal Space Program* and *Stellaris* to Spore.

## 1.15 Notable gaps and uncertainties
- No official **lifetime sales** total was found. The best figures are EA's "over two million" (May 2009) and EEDAR's ~2.8M estimate.
- **Budget:** only the 2006 "$20M projected" report, which this research could not read directly, and loose estimates.
- **DRM lawsuits:** no outcome found.
- **Small date conflicts:**
  - Steam launch: 19 vs 22 Dec 2008.
  - Creature Creator: 17 vs 18 June 2008, and $9.95 vs $9.99.
  - Johnson joined Maxis: April vs May 2007.
  - Bot Parts promotion end: 2010 vs 2011.
  - Number of GDC 2005 phases: 6 vs 7.
- **Unverified:** no source linked *Star Control*, Richard Dawkins (directly by Wright), or Kees van Prooijen to Spore's development. *Katamari Damacy* appears only as a reference on Wright's GDC 2005 design graph.
